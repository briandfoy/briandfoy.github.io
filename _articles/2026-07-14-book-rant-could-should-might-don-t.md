I tried to read Nick Foster's *Could Should Might Don't*, but it is so hopelessly uninformed, naive, and self-congratulatory in his alternate reality that I just couldn't; I shouldn't; I won't; so I didn't. This is the sort of beach read book enjoyed by those who don't really care what it says as long as they are mildly surprised about something that didn't know existed and wouldn't care much it if didn't.

<!--more-->

The job of the critic, and I have my critic hat on now, is to figure out what the artist was trying to do and judge, within those limits, if the artist met that intent. So often, the artist has no idea that compels them and presented whatever they ended up with. There's nothing they have in mind to judge their own work.

I can't really figure out what Nick wanted, and I suspect he doesn't know himself. He says that he wanted to figure out how to think about the future, but also says that thinking about the future is pointless because it is likely to be wrong. He says that he doesn't want to create a specific framework, but then creates a simplistic framework. He leans on his résumé, to the point that it's almost the fallacy of the appeal to authority.

I suspect that he's trying to work out a mid-life, post career crisis where he looks behind him and finds nothing. He had some fun times talking about inconsequential things (speculative futurism), ultimately was unsatisfied, and now needs to convince himself that he has led a good life. I don't think he accomplishes that, but he does have an ax to grind.

Mostly, he despises science fiction despite a very limited view of what it is. I think his ideas come from big budget movies, and much less from the literature behind them. History apparently started in 1961 when Kennedy instructed  "ask not what your country can do for you", or maybe slightly later when *Star Trek* first aired. Anything earlier is prehistoric not only in memory, but in attitude. Wouldn't design be so easy if we could just ignore people? Much of speculative design, after all, is thought divorced from reality and utility as a way to explore some tangential problem.

He has a palpable stick in his craw about science fiction and his own role in the world and the lack of deference he imagines the world shows him or the value he thinks he offers. Don Draper's line is best: when an employee pokes him with "I feel sorry for you", he says "I don't think of you at all". You haven't given us any reason to think of you, and I don't know if Nick existed before this book. This could be an elaborate prank.

Nick apparently attributes his debasement to science fiction, which he imagines is all about gadgets and mechanical materialism, when much of it is social and political allegory. He tries hard to justify his existence and to convince the reader that he's worthwhile, much in the same way you have to listen to your drunk friend explain why it's not his fault he crashed his car. Look, I get it, shut up and move on.

We only think future is about the future, though, and like that drunk friend, it's about elaborate, disguised explanations of how we explain the past and how we'd do it differently. Indeed, *Star Trek* was a progressive show about current events, and that it was in space with the shiny thing they used to distract standards and practices. That show had the first televised interracial kiss!

The first third of the book is a long-winded, repetitive, messily-organized set of premises and misdirections that you have to accept before he can make an argument, and if you can't accept those, which you can't, you must reject everything that follows. I kept thinking I had missed a page or accidentally re-read a page, but no, it's just a mess of dog projectile vomiting, letting the chunks land where they may. And then, that dog eating that vomit again to have a second go at the tired ideas.

These premises are the sort of thing inexperienced college students debate in the weeks before their first-year classes start because no one listening has the knowledge to refute the ideas or the willingness to offend a potential sex partner or weed supplier. Those kids have those thoughts because they have yet to do anything that informs them. They've lived their lives being fed things by teachers and books, and might still believe the high school threat of "this is going on your permanent record", or that anyone cares what their college grades will be. Indeed, in many schools, especially the Ivys, you are going to get an A no matter what you do.

So, who is Nick Foster, and why does he think we should listen to him? The beginning of the book is everything that makes you decide to move to the other side of the room during a party.

According to [his own website](https://www.nickfosterrdi.com):

> As Head of Design at Google X, he led a team of designers, researchers and prototypers developing nascent technologies such as brain-controlled computer interfaces, intelligent robotics, stratospheric internet balloons and neighborhood-scale nuclear fusion."

Working at Google isn't the pip any Googler thinks it is (they have a name for themselves, although Ex-Googlers are Xooglers. I'm not even joking.). It hasn't been like that for years.

Every design I've seen from Google, aside from the original search page, has been frustrating, incomplete, and not fit for purpose. And ugly. And joyless. I feel a little part of my soul decaying as I have to interact with the dumb ideas Google tried to force on us. I'm mostly excised their dreadful influence and feel so much better for it.

I've visited several Google campuses, and while they are curious, they are more like pre-schools than workplaces, and I think I've asked my host at each of them "Is anyone actually working?" To be fair, I know Googlers who do work, but you don't see them at the fancy recreational facilities, because they are working. They don't have eclectically decorated workspaces, or don't hang out in the various nooks and crannies. They're working.

At some conference, the summer Google tried to make Google+ a thing ("stop trying to make fetch happen"), the Googler I was chatting with could not comprehend the idea that anything you say in a "private" "Circle" is private. Anyone who sees it can repeat it, anywhere they choose to. The technology has no effect on that. People thought Snapchat pictures could not be copied, but if my eyes can see it, so can the camera on my second phone. These are not hard things to imagine.

That's Google: a cohort of overpaid, self-congratulatory, self-assured, uninformed technocrats, accomplishing nothing, or worse, actively destroying society. That's the environment that forms the first think Nick boasts. Not something he's done, or something we use, but who was paying him. Not only that, he says he "led", but in every other interview I listened to, he basically says he wandered around, landing nowhere. Save the puffery for the job applications.

So, what has Nick ever designed? What has he actually done that changed anything? I've read so many profiles of him and I've yet to find one that mentions anything that I would have seen, much less cared about.  [The Observer says](https://observer.co.uk/news/science-technology/article/nick-foster-predictions-are-mostly-nonsense):

> his job involved speaking to staff from technical backgrounds – computer scientists, roboticists, biologists, and “asking a lot of dumb questions” to understand what a nascent technology could do.

But that's not design. That's not anything. That's just shitting on whatever is in front of you with no prior thought about it. This seems a job better suited to a behavioral economist. "What are the incentives and how will people react?" Designers, no matter how much they esteem themselves, don't change what people want. Johnny Ive didn't change what people want by giving them the iPhone. They don't want iPhones; they use the iPhone to pursue (or not) what they want.

*The Observer* continues:

> When I ask, however, about how ethical concerns he raises in the book should be applied to real-world workplaces, he is reluctant to venture into specifics.

So, what dumb questions was he asking? Did he even write this book? Why can't he speak to something he put in the book he wants people to give him money to read?

It continues:

> When I ask whether tech companies should invest in the future by paying their fair share of tax, he says the topic is “very tricky territory for me”, and “totally out of my wheelhouse”.

But, wasn't every topic in front of you out of your wheelhouse? You were willing to talk all sorts of nonsense on all sorts of things you'd heard about five minutes ago, so what's different? Wasn't that the point of putting the Connecticut Yankee in the court? Wasn't the idea of speculative futurism exploring ideas? Isn't this less about solutions and more about perspectives? These are softball questions.

All of this begs the question that you can you call yourself a designer without your ideas ever materializing, without having to ever explore anything in depth, and without ever showing your work? It's the idea that matters, not the practicality. Is it even design if it does not achieve some goal, even if it is intentionally hostile design?

Every half-drunk, chatty guy on a barstool is just as much a speculative futurist as Nick Foster. We can all design Martian cities, flying cars, and systems of government, and indeed, regular, not-fêted people are doing that speculative design work every day, half in the bag, for free. With no accountability, my idea of bike lanes is just as good as yours. So why did Google X pay Nick? To hear him say "this is not in my wheelhouse?". Or, is his wheelhouse spouting shit only for pay?

In one of his interviews on the [Design Better](https://designbetterpodcast.com/p/nick-foster)  podcast, the host mentions that Foster has worked on over 200 projects. That's also not the flex anyone thinks it is. It means Foster never had to spend that much time on anything. There's only one comment on the [YouTube version](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U-JsOBa-DAA) of that podcast:

> Wow. I don't know what to make of the fact that 1.3k views and 4 months later, nobody had anything to say about this interview.

Or, consider [Nick's interview with iF Design magazine](https://ifdesign.com/en/if-magazine/industrial-designer-nick-foster-future-thinking) (or on [YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4gd_ccRYgrw), with almost no views and zero comments, again). I won't repeat his answer to "why did you write this book" because it's 150+ word non-answer. But consider the follow-up question where the interviewer tires to save him by asking "So, you decided to write a book on how to think about the future in a good way?", which includes him saying:

> After being in the workspace for so long, I started having that internal conversation about how we should think about the future.

Wait. What were you doing before? Isn't this your thing? Weren't you paid to do this before? What were you doing in that career that is now behind you?

But, he also says in that same answer:

> So I started to write things down. It evolved and evolved and evolved, and it became unwieldy. And so it turned into a book.

Yeah, I noticed. Making it a book didn't solve that problem. It gets worse though:

> I've always found writing to be a way of making sense of the world for myself.

That's true enough. But when you want to sell that book, you have to make sense to the readers too. I write plenty of stuff that I abandon because I've reached the level of understanding I think the topic deserves. That doesn't mean it's good enough to show other people. And, isn't that the point of design?

I watch other interviews, but he says the same thing everywhere, and none of it is impressive. None of it gives me any idea on why he has anything interesting to say about anything.

Now, you might say Foster can't tell us what he did because he is bound by a non-disclosure agreement. And, these FAANG companies are well-known black holes where your friends go to work and disappear for several years. But really, if you are inducted as a Royal Designer for Industry, surely there's some body of work to point to.

But, if the things that he designed are so secret, do any of us care? If these things never changed our lives, they might as well not exist. And, if they had changed our lives, someone would have spilled the beans and there would be some sniff of credit somewhere on the internet. Some disgruntled ex-Googler with his computing idea would have added an alt Reddit account to his stable to complain about Foster's idea.

Oh wait, I solved cancer, but I signed an NDA.

Jony Ive, like his countryman Foster, is a Royal Designer for Industry, a group of 200 (and no more) designers in the United Kingdom. Ive is listed under "Product Design". We know what he did, whether we like it or not. The design of the MacBook and the iPhone are basically what they were when he left Apple.

Other RDI fellows are listed under "Engineering", "Graphic", "Sound", or one of many other types. Then there are other curious groups: "Speculative Design" (Anab Jain), "Multidiscipline Design" (Yinka Ilori), and then "Futures Design" (Nick Foster). The most concrete of all these in Ilori is making patterns for North Face clothing. He didn't design the clothing or invent anything new. He did what people have done for hundreds of years: make patterns on clothes. Did he invent the process, sew the designs, or anything else? Not that he says. The pattern is the featured achievement on his own website.

But what do these sorts of people do? They write books with their untried and untested ideas, thinking that their lack of empiricism somehow makes them worth listening to. They give interviews, they give paid talks, and they graciously serve as foci for adulation that might otherwise be wasted.

I think the best measure of this class of people is the TED talks, which started as a good thing with insanely focused and talented people with decades of experience working in the dark corners of human knowledge.

But, TED did what all such things do: they quickly ran out of people with something interesting to say, enough experience to say it, and the stage presence to hold an audience. The entire thing turned into a way to meet Al Gore, who, for some reason, people want to meet, and you just have to sit in the dark listening to some asshole spout bullshit before you can break for coffee and cookies again. Look into the number of celebrated talks that turned out to be fake. Start with the Power Pose. It doesn't matter that it was all bullshit, because Al Gore.

Then, what is "Could Should Might Don't"? The idea that there are things we could do, should do, might do, and shouldn't do is nothing new. That is, essentially, the entire point of philosophy. For thousands of years, groups of people have debated what we know, what we value, and how we should behave based on that.

Foster apparently doesn't see the oddity of talking about the idea of "protopia", a modern term, while using "utopia" and "dystopia" without attribution or explanation, much to the detriment of any idea he might be trying to tease out of whatever he thinks he is thinking. It's not at all clear to me that he has any new idea, and his ragged survey of world history seems drawn entirely from an encyclopedia, with its shallow, trite repetition of third-grade book reports.

And god damn, for a guy who hates science fiction so much, he sure wants to talk about it a lot. Show me on the doll where science fiction touched you, Nick. But, it's not science fiction, really. It's political and social commentary hidden from the normies who are distracted by the laser blasters. And a lot of it is written by people who think they should be in charge and would have done communism right, where in reality, the historians know these same people would have been shot in some alley by Stalin's henchmen. Well, not in an alley. It would be right there on the street.

For a book about a speculative future, Nick spends most of his page count throwing around half-explained past thinking that doesn't hold up even under the most lenient scrutiny. Get off the Google campus and have a real street taco. I can imagine that some 21-year-old wanks off to this shit and repeats it while hitting the bong back in their dorm room, but the people who get things done don't have time to listen to some uniformed prattle for the new kid on his first day at work who thinks he has a better idea for everything.

And here is the problem. If you've escaped Big Tech, you already understand how Foster has gone so wrong. That alternate reality is full of potentially smart people trapped in a bubble where they are told they are smart and have never failed. Every move has been up the ladder, and never down, which means they've never had to be that creative or dedicated. People who have never had the heavy foot of reality rest on their necks don't have the capacity to adapt to the hostile world that wants to kill them. They are insulated by a system that protects and coddles them, tells them they are smart, tells them they should run the world, and lets them design an electric car whose doors can't open when the electrical system fails or the handle ices over. It's not actually that bleak; the Tesla has a hidden pull cable to open the door, but it doesn't disclose that to the Uber rider who just got in the car.

How about stick vacuums, like the ones you could get from Dyson, another place Nick has worked (his first real job for a year)? Who designed those with batteries that would wear out in three years and could not be replaced? Certainly not the tool brands you find in Home Depot, where all the tools in a line use the same batteries and can't be changed because then all of your tools would become useless? And, even if you could change the battery, the cost of another one is almost the entire cost of the product, unless you get the dodgy Chinese version that might burn down your house.

Think about every other futuristic thing you have ever tried to use because some tech company tried to make it the next thing. The Oculus or the Apple Vision Pro? Who thought it would be a good thing to strap a three-pound battery on someone's head? You know who would never suggest that? A soldier who has had to wear night vision all night and now has a 30% disability rating from the VA because of it.

Whose idea was Google Glass, a bad idea now resurrected as Meta Ray-Bans? Psychopaths, literal psychopaths, think these are a good idea that society should scramble to get. They are now widely regarded as perv- or creep-googles. And, guess who is watching it all? [Teams of low-paid African workers](https://9to5mac.com/2026/03/03/meta-ray-ban-smart-glasses-send-sensitive-videos-to-human-data-annotators/). Dude, I could have told you that for free. Maybe Nick did bring that up the inevitable privacy questions, but remember, so many things aren't in his wheelhouse.

A more thoughtful, actual futurist, Nicholas Negroponte, pointed out that anything you do to improve an interface just moves the physical stress to something you haven't thought about yet. The eye strain from these VR headsets is going to make us all blind, and not because we're wanking while watching porn on them. You'd already know that if you'd ever seen *The Jerk*. Or, maybe you do know if and don't care, because as they say on the Street as one trader sells junk to a buyer who knows it's junk: "I won't be here, you won't be there".

Futurists never seem to account for the passage of time or the fickleness of the supply chain. As Homer Simpson puts it:

> That’s a problem for future Homer. Man, I don’t envy that guy!

You know what people want in the future? The same thing they want right now: A vacuum cleaner that lasts for 40 years, a washing machine that lasts 40 years, a refrigerator that lasts 40 years, and a TV that lasts for 40 years.

You know who doesn't want that? Someone who moves every three years and gets new fixtures because the landlord or sellers just replaced everything. Or people who don't use their fixtures because someone else supplies their food on demand, washes their clothes, and takes care of everything else to the extent that the person has nothing interesting to say. I don't know how Nick lives, but a designer should strive to have as many experiences in as many situations as they can fit into life so they understand as much of the breadth of life as they can shove into the short time they have to figure out how to think and, well, design.

But there's a term for modern, intentional design, for whom I'd call an actual, near-term futurist: Cory Doctorow. He invented the term "enshittification" in which these huge tech companies actually plan to make your life worse in the hopes of extracting money and rents from you. I think the designers know what they are doing and the harm they are causing, but the money salves all their misgivings and pains. I bet if you pay Nick enough, he'll come up with a way to make everyone's life worse. I bet he already has.

Compare this to the backlash to AI now, and how the people who work there must know that people hate them, but they console themselves that they will quit as soon as, well, the next thing really, but no, about to vest, so after that, but no, just another year. Rage quitting AI companies seem to be the way to get a raise and a promotion. Get yours while getting is good.

Consider how many people inside Theranos knew it was a scam, but they sold their souls anyway, and how quickly the titans of politics lined up to crush the whistleblowers, even if one of those whistleblowers was their grandson. These people might not be shoving people into ovens, but they are certainly driving the trains that take them there. The banality of evil is well paid.

So what is Nick even on about? I don't think even he knows. I think the best summary of this book was a riff on Mitch Hedburg: "I used to think futurists were stupid. I still do, but I used to too."
